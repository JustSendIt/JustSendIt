01

What this is

JustSendIt is a website built around two joke cryptocurrencies called $SEND and $GWC. A joke cryptocurrency, usually called a memecoin, is a coin made for fun and community rather than for solving a problem. Most of them lose all their value. That is not a warning buried in small print here; it is the starting assumption the whole site is designed around.

The site does five things. It explains how to buy the coins if you have never bought crypto before. It gives people a place to hang out, post, and follow each other. It runs a points game that rewards showing up and taking part. It scans coins — any coin on Robinhood Chain, Ethereum or Solana, and brand-new ones on Robinhood Chain as they appear — and flags the ones that look dangerous. And it shows the market: figures for chains and tokens from public sources, each one saying where it came from.

Here is the important part. The site never holds your money, never asks for your password to a wallet, and never tells you to buy anything. It is a scoreboard and a clubhouse, not a bank and not a broker.

The one-sentence version

A clubhouse for two joke coins, with a points game attached and a smoke detector pointed at new coins — where nothing you do can be paid for, and nothing the site says is advice.

What it refuses to say

Most crypto websites are adverts. This one is built with a short list of things it is not allowed to claim, and those rules show up in the code and the copy, not just in an about page.

  • It never says a coin will go up. It shows what the chain says and lets you decide.
  • It never claims to be unhackable. No site can honestly promise that, so it does not.
  • It is not connected to Robinhood the company. The chain is called Robinhood Chain. That is where the name comes from and nothing more.
  • It calls itself entertainment. Points, levels and games are for fun. They are not money and cannot be exchanged for money.
  • It never inflates a number. If the site cannot verify something, it says so instead of guessing.

04

Getting an account

Reading is open. Joining takes a ticket.

You need to tick one box to read this site: that you are 18 or older. It is asked once per device, before the page can be seen (on the front page, the moment the loading screen ends), and remembered for a year. Saying you are under 18 keeps the site closed. The site will not make an account without that tick, and it records on the account when it was given. The privacy and terms pages are the one exception and open without it. After that you do not need anything to read. The front page, the wall, the risk scanner, the competitions, other people's profiles and every Send Call are open to anyone who visits, signed in or not. A few things are behind a sign-in. The New Pairs radar on the Scanner page (section 13) is for members: scanning any one address is open to everyone, the live feed of brand-new pools is not. And each community's holders-only wall (section 11) can only be read by its verified holders. Every Send Squad (section 11b) is for signed-in members too, and so is the wallet tracker (section 7). The pages listed above are open to anyone, and search engines may read every one of them. A member's profile is only offered to search results once they have chosen their own @username and put at least one public post on their wall; until then the page asks search engines to leave it out.

You do need an invite code to make an account. Someone already inside sends you one of theirs, and the moment you try to sign up — from any page — a ticket headed Ticket to Send 🚀 opens over whatever you were reading. It states how to get in before you type anything — a code from someone already inside, ten codes of your own once you are in, and the read-only $100 of $SEND wallet check that full access takes — then you enter the code and make your account. Your own codes last as long as your account does: delete it, and the ones you have not given out stop working.

You can always close it. Every step of the ticket has a way out that says so plainly, and closing it puts you back exactly where you were, still reading. A door you cannot walk away from is a wall, and this site does not put one in front of people who are only looking.

Four details worth stating:

  • Each code works once. When you get in you are given ten of your own to hand out, which is how the room fills up.
  • Your Send ID is your place in line — the number you were when you joined. It is fixed the instant your account exists and never changes. It is printed on your ticket, which you can download as a picture with your name and profile photo on it.
  • A code you have already handed out is shown to you as used, and cannot be copied again. The site does not even show you the rest of its characters, because there is nothing to copy: it will never work a second time, and sending a friend a dead code is worse than sending none.
  • Three accounts per connection. The site counts how many accounts have been made from one internet connection in the last 90 days, and refuses a fourth. A household sharing one line fits inside that; a ring of fake accounts does not. If several people really do share a connection, one account can link up to five wallets, and every one of them counts toward the same Send Power. The connection itself is kept only as a fingerprint (Section 5), and that fingerprint is erased 90 days after the account was made.

Once you have a ticket, you can join with a crypto wallet or with an email and password. The code can also sign people in through Google, Facebook, X or Instagram, but each of those appears only when whoever runs the site has switched it on with that company’s app keys; one that is not switched on is not offered at all. So the sign-in window always shows three ways in — 🦊 Connect wallet, 🔥 Burn Verification (for a wallet that cannot sign) and ✉️ Email — and a social button appears beside them only once it is switched on. The invite is checked at every way in — and at nothing else on the site.

You pick your @username as you join. It is your public name, and you can sign in with it as well as with your email. Joining with a wallet asks for the name first, before the wallet connects, and can also take an email and password as a second way in — optional, and useful if you ever want to sign in without the wallet. Both forms offer to set up two-factor straight afterwards; that is optional too, and for the first hour of a new account nothing in its settings asks for a password. If the email you give already belongs to another account, the sign-up is not refused — a refusal would tell anyone with a ticket who is a member. Your account is made without that email: you sign in with your @username and password, and can set a different email later.

Making an account, and then using it

These are two different things here, and the difference is the point.

An email is enough to make an account. No wallet, no signature, no coins. You can read every page, check in each day, and set up your own profile — your name, bio and colours — though that earns no points until the check below passes. Pictures are uploads like any other and wait for the check.

A wallet is what lets you do things. Posting, calling, commenting, reacting, voting, joining a community — all of it waits behind one read-only check, which opens the moment you try, over whatever page you were on. It asks two questions of the chain, across every wallet you have linked:

  • Do you hold at least $100 of $SEND? Valued at the lower of the live price and the price the $SEND pool actually sat at over the last 24 hours — its time-weighted median, rebuilt from the pool’s own reserves — so a minute-long pump cannot buy anyone through the door; a pump has to hold for half a day before it moves that median at all. If the live price is more than three times that median, or the chain cannot be read, the site refuses to value the bag and nothing is decided. A dust balance is not a holding, and there has to be a market buy behind the bag: a wallet that has only ever received $SEND from somewhere else has not bought any.
  • Are you a net seller? Of the coins that moved between you and the market, no more may have gone out than came in.

And one question of the site's own records: has this wallet already let someone else in? A wallet can open the door for one account only. The moment it verifies an account it is remembered — as a keyed hash, never the address itself — and linking it to a second account will not verify that one, however much it holds. Unlinking the wallet does not release it, and neither does deleting the account it verified. Otherwise one $100 bag could be linked, used, unlinked and passed along forever, and the check would be counting the same coins as many times as anyone cared to move them.

There is no waiting period, and the first day is watched instead. The check used to ask a third question — whether you had held for over a week — and it no longer does. The door opens the moment you hold the bag. In exchange, for 24 hours after your most recent market buy, the position that opened the door has to still be there: let that balance drop by more than about 2% inside that window (a partial sale and a transfer out count the same; the 2% exists because a transfer fee or a dust rounding is not a sell) and it counts as a strike on the same ladder as every other anti-cheat trigger (Section 15): read-only for a day the first time, a week the second, for good the third, bought out at $25 of $SEND per day of the sanction and doubled if that bought bag is sold before its hold is up. The window is anchored on the buy rather than on the sign-up, so somebody who has held since long before today is never in one at all. A week of waiting asked everybody to prove patience before they had done anything; this asks only the person who actually dumps to answer for it.

What $100 actually means on $SEND. At the market prices of 13 September 2026, when this version was written, $100 was about 0.027% of $SEND’s ten billion supply, so on the order of 3,763 wallets could clear this bar at the same time. ($GWC is far tighter — one billion tokens at a market value then around $11,650 made $100 roughly 0.86% of every $GWC in existence, about 116 wallets — which is why the gate no longer asks for it. That floor still applies to the OG badge and to Diamond status, where it is doing a different job.) These are the only figures in this paper that move: they follow the price, and the site values the check at the lower of the live price and the median of the last 24 hours, so a momentary spike cannot buy anyone through the door. The number is a deliberate choice made with the arithmetic in hand, not a round figure picked without checking what it buys.

The check itself is not a punishment. Read-only mode (Section 15) is a penalty with strikes and an escalating ladder. This is simply a new account that has not shown its hand yet, and the site says so in those words. The first-day sell window above is the one part of it that is a penalty, and it is worded as what it is: a strike on that ladder.

Nothing about it can move your money. You sign a sentence to prove the wallet is yours. That signature moves nothing, approves nothing, and costs no gas. The check never asks a wallet to send anything. The only transactions this site ever builds are the swaps in Section 7, and each one waits for you to approve it in your own wallet. A wallet that cannot sign (a GMGN wallet, say) is proved another way: within an hour, you send any amount of $SEND or ETH — as much or as little as you like — from it to the burn address yourself, in your own wallet app. That is the one proof where something leaves the wallet, by your own hand, and it is gone for good; the site only reads it on the chain, and an account that uses it signs in with an email and a password, because a burn cannot sign anyone in. The transaction has to be the wallet’s own — an app holding an allowance cannot make one for it — and one burn proves one account: if burn windows from more than one account are open on the same wallet when it lands, it links none of them, and they are all told.

Your history is read from the chain itself. Every transfer of a token is recorded on the blockchain as a log, so the site reads your wallet’s whole $SEND history — everything it ever sent and received — straight from the chain, and checks that it adds up to your wallet’s balance before deciding anything. A block explorer is asked first only when the site has been given an API key for one; the public explorer meets servers with a bot challenge, and the site does not try to get past it, so without a key the chain answers directly (Section 16b). Signing in with a wallet starts the check on its own.

A buy is a buy, whichever app carried it. Most swaps do not come straight from the pool: a router or an aggregator takes the pool’s coins and passes them on to you. So the site asks the chain what the pool itself did in the same transaction. If the pool sent $SEND out, the coins that reached you count as bought — but never more than the pool actually sent, so a contract cannot forward coins from its own stock and call them a purchase. Selling is read the same way in reverse. A transfer from another person’s wallet, with no pool movement behind it, is still not a buy.

If the chain cannot be read, nothing is decided. The check can fail to complete — the public node throttles bursts of reads. When that happens the site says nothing has been decided and asks again by itself every fifteen minutes, rather than recording a refusal it did not earn. Answers that only need your history — no balance, nothing bought from the market, more sold than bought — are given even while the price cannot be read.

Once you pass, the rest of your profile is yours to shape: claim your handle, and then, entirely optionally, add an email and password, turn on two-factor, and link more wallets, up to five in all. A wallet-only account is a complete account. (An account that proved a wallet by a burn already has an email and password: a burn cannot sign you in.)

Signing in with a wallet

A wallet is an app that holds your coins. Signing in with one does not hand it over. The site shows you a sentence and asks your wallet to sign it, the way you might sign your name to prove it is you. Signing a sentence moves nothing and gives permission for nothing.

The sentence has the website's own address written inside it. That matters. If a scammer copies this site onto a lookalike domain, the sentence their fake site shows will name their address, so an alert reader can spot the mismatch before signing. The site also invents a fresh random code for each attempt, so an old signature cannot be reused later.

It is written in the exact “Sign-In with Ethereum” layout, which is why it looks the way it does: a wallet that recognises the format shows it as a sign-in request and warns you when the site asking does not match the domain named inside the text.

Signing in with a password

Passwords are never stored. They are put through a slow scrambling process called scrypt, which is deliberately hard for a computer to run millions of times, so guessing is expensive. Only the scrambled result is kept. Passwords are also capped at 1024 characters so nobody can jam the server with an enormous one.

Two-factor

You can add a second lock in one of three ways. One is a six-digit code from an authenticator app that changes every 30 seconds. Another is a wallet signature — from the one linked wallet you chose when you turned it on; wallets linked afterwards do not count, so a stolen session cannot add a fresh wallet and use that. And an account that started with a wallet and later added an email and password can use that password as the second factor for wallet sign-ins. Turning any two-factor off, or changing which wallet is the key, requires the current factor — at the change, or at the “confirm it's you” step within the last 30 minutes (section 6) — so someone who steals a logged-in session cannot remove it unless they took it inside that window. Code guessing is rate-limited and gives up after five tries, which destroys the half-finished login.

Sign in with Send

Other people’s apps and games can let members sign in with their Send account, the way many sites offer a Google or X button. It uses the standard protocols for this, OAuth 2.0 with PKCE and OpenID Connect, so a builder can use an ordinary library. It is run by this site’s own server, not by a blockchain or a group of servers: this site is the one that says who you are. What it hands an app is a signed note (an ID token, signed with a key published at /.well-known/jwks.json), so the app can check the note without asking this site again.

What an app can see

When an app asks for something you have not answered yet, this site shows you a page with the app’s name, its homepage, where it will send you back, and “Not reviewed by Just Send It” until an admin has looked at it. Below that is one tick-box for each thing the app asked for, most showing your own value, and every box starts unticked:

  • Your handle and your picture.
  • Your Send ID — the same number in every app you share it with, which is the point: one account, the same everywhere. Send IDs go in the order members joined, and join dates are public on the Wall, so it can also narrow down who you are. Its box says both.
  • Your Send Power, with your level and title.
  • Your Send Boost — the total only. It is made from your holdings, your OG tier, a live community and the Rocket Run, weekly and beta boosts, so an app can estimate your holdings from it, and its box says that too.
  • Whether your wallet passed the holder check.
  • Keeping these up to date for up to 30 days after you sign in, without a new sign-in.

Apart from what you tick and a private ID for you, an app is sent nothing, except, when it asks how recent your sign-in is, a time worked out from its own question. Apps never get your email, your wallets, your sign-in history or your communities. Each app gets its own random id for you, made the first time you allow it, so two apps cannot match you up by that id. Your picture and exact Send Power are public on the Wall next to your handle, and your Send ID follows the order members joined, so an app given any of them can find or narrow down your handle; their boxes say so.

Guarding the door

The first time you allow an app, the site asks you to confirm it’s you (Section 6), unless you have just signed in for it or your session is still unlocked, so someone who copied your session cannot connect an app with one press unless they took it inside that window. Changing your password, ending your other sessions or turning two-factor off ends every app’s sign-in with Send, and each one asks you again next time, as a restriction or deleting your account does. When an app asks you to prove it’s you again (a fresh sign-in), you are asked for that alone, and what you allowed it stays as it was. Settings → Apps you’ve signed in to lists every app, what it can see and since when, and Revoke ends it.

Every app must use PKCE, which stops a stolen sign-in code from being used by anyone else. Codes last 60 seconds and work once; an access pass lasts an hour; a keep-up-to-date pass is swapped for a new one each time it is used, and using an old one ends the whole chain. Limits are counted per member, per sign-in and per address, and an app that runs on a server has budgets of its own that only its own successful requests spend, so no member, no crowd of accounts and no stream of refused requests can use up an app’s share.

What is kept

The site keeps each app’s registration; for each member and app, what was ticked and which boxes were asked, when it was first allowed and when that answer last changed, and the app’s id for that member; and the live codes and passes, as one-way fingerprints, until soon after they expire (they are left out of backups). It tells an app when you signed in only if the app asks how recent your sign-in is, and then gives a time worked out from the question (or the exact second of a fresh sign-in you make for that app), never one read off your session, so apps cannot match you up by it; an app that keeps asking could still narrow your last sign-in down to about ten minutes. For an app an admin switched off, it also keeps the host names that app sent members to, so no other app can use them until the admin lifts that. It keeps no log of which app you used when, and not your internet address or device, beyond rate-limit counters kept in memory for up to about an hour.

For builders

The Developers page has the addresses, the full list of what each permission shares, a browser example in about thirty lines, a server example, the button, and Your apps: up to five apps per member, each with up to five return addresses. An admin can mark an app reviewed or switch it off. The site’s operator can also set up one app in advance, the game Sendy’s Metaverse at sendy.gg, without a member registering it.

05

Scrambling your data

Everything personal is stored scrambled, using a method called AES-256-GCM. Think of it as a safe that also has a tamper seal: without the key you cannot read what is inside, and if anyone alters the contents the seal breaks and the site refuses to trust it.

The key lives outside the database. In a proper deployment it is set as an environment variable. On a developer's machine the server makes one and writes it to a file only the owner can read. The key can also be rotated: a new key is added, every scrambled value — in the live database and in every backup — is scrambled again under it, and the old key is dropped only once nothing still needs it.

Your preference settings are scrambled the same way. What is not scrambled is what is already public — usernames, posts, comments, calls and the boards — because searching, sorting and drawing every wall needs to read it, and scrambling it would hide nothing.

The Data API — one door, one key

Anyone who wants the site’s data in bulk — for an AI, a bot, a spreadsheet — goes through one door. A year of access is minted for an account whose linked wallets have sent at least its price — $1,000 of $SEND, half for OG Silver, three quarters for OG Bronze, nothing for OG Gold — to the burn address, checked on the blockchain and valued at the lower of the live price and the day’s median, so a momentary spike cannot inflate it. A key lasts a year and spends the tokens that were worth that price on the day; renewing takes another burn and adds a year. One burn backs one key at a time. OG Gold pays nothing and never expires while the badge is live; OG Silver pays half; OG Bronze pays three quarters. Burning is permanent and buys nothing but the key; the site does not tell anyone to do it.

The key opens everything that is already public, in bulk, and everything in the holder’s own account, unscrambled for them. It never opens anyone else’s private data — a burn does not change whose data it is. The key itself is shown once and stored only as a fingerprint, so a stolen database does not contain it. The same key also opens what Market Data collects (Section 13b) — chains, tokens, pools, the RWAs and the bubble map — in the same shape the page uses, an unknown still an unknown.

The clever bit: looking things up without unlocking them

If your email is scrambled, how does the site find your account when you log in? It keeps a second value called a blind index: a fixed fingerprint of your email made with a secret key. Two identical emails always make the same fingerprint, so the site can match you, but the fingerprint cannot be turned back into your email.

What a thief would actually see — stated precisely

Someone who copied the database without the key would find meaningless text starting with a version tag such as v1: or v2: where the emails, linked wallets and two-factor secrets should be. That much is genuinely protected.

But it is not true that the file would tell them nothing. Usernames, profiles, every post and comment, the follow and mute graph, each account's $SEND and $GWC balances, and the dollar amounts spent on calls are all stored in plain form, because most of it is public on the site anyway. One place can still show a wallet address in plain text next to an account, and only by choice: a Send Call, when the caller ticks the box to share a wallet on it. The box is off by default, the address can be taken off the call again later even though the call itself is permanent, and calls made before that choice existed had their address removed. The log of points awarded used to carry addresses; it now stores only keyed fingerprints, and the old entries were rewritten.

So the honest summary is that the most sensitive fields are encrypted and the rest of the database is readable. And if the key were stolen along with the data, even the encrypted part would be open — which is why the key belongs somewhere the database backups are not.

What is kept when something changes

A member once lost a Conviction Play to one stray tap, and with it the date and the price it was pinned at: nothing kept an earlier copy, and the daily backups reached back a week. So the site now keeps a copy of a row as it was, before every change that matters and before every deletion, for these things: Conviction Plays, posts, comments (on posts and on a Sent It), Send Calls, your profile (name, picture, bio, colours and handles), your Diamond Hands streak, your community and squad memberships, your watchlist, your follows and your post alerts.

Those copies are kept for as long as the account exists — the newest 5,000 per member — and deleting the account erases them with everything else. Only the site’s admin can see them, and can put one entry back. Some things are never copied: anything scrambled or looked up by a key, anything used to sign in, IP addresses and wallets. Nor is anything a moderator takes down: a takedown erases the copies its own change just wrote, so abusive content is gone rather than one tap from coming back.

06

Locking the doors

Web attacks mostly work by getting a browser to run code it should not, or by tricking your browser into doing something while you are logged in. The site closes those doors with rules the browser itself enforces.

The strict list

A Content Security Policy is a list the site hands your browser saying where code and images may come from. This one is unusually tight: scripts may only load from the site itself and the analytics hosts the policy names, and there is no inline script anywhere on any page. If an attacker somehow slipped a script tag into a post, the browser would refuse to run it.

default-src 'self' script-src 'self' https://static.cloudflareinsights.com ← our own code, plus Cloudflare's analytics beacon (+ www.googletagmanager.com, only while Google Analytics is turned on) style-src 'self' 'unsafe-inline' font-src 'self' ← our own copies of the fonts img-src 'self' data: blob: ← token art comes through our server (+ Google Analytics' hosts, only while it is turned on) media-src 'self' blob: data: ← video and voice posts, served from this site connect-src 'self' https://rpc.mainnet.chain.robinhood.com https://cloudflareinsights.com ← the swap and wallet flows, and the beacon's report (+ Google Analytics' collection hosts, only while it is turned on) frame-src https://dexscreener.com https://www.geckoterminal.com ← a pool's chart from them, only when you pick it base-uri 'self' form-action 'self' ← forms may only submit to this site frame-ancestors 'none' ← nobody can hide us in their page object-src 'none'

Beside Cloudflare's analytics beacon — and Google Analytics' tag, only while the operator has it turned on (the privacy policy, section 7, says what it sends) — pages load nothing from other companies: the fonts are served from this site, and token pictures, prices, market figures and wallet history are fetched by our server and passed on, so Dexscreener, GeckoTerminal, CoinGecko and the image hosts never see a visitor's address (and the block explorer's API is not asked at all unless the site has a key for it, Section 16b). frame-ancestors 'none' stops a scammer loading the real site invisibly inside a fake one, an attack called clickjacking. frame-src names exactly two sites, dexscreener.com and www.geckoterminal.com: the site draws its price charts itself from each pair contract's own swap events, and a viewer can switch a token's chart to Dexscreener's or CoinGecko's — a frame that is only loaded when they pick it, offered only when our server has confirmed that site lists the pool.

Checking who asked

Whenever something changes — a post, a call, a setting — the server checks which website the request came from and rejects it unless it came from this one. That stops another site from acting for you while you happen to be logged in.

Confirming it's you, once

A change to how you get into your account — adding an email or a wallet (your first wallet too), changing your password or email, turning two-factor on or off, unlinking a wallet or disconnecting them all (whether two-factor is on or not), making a data key, deleting the account — needs proof that you are the owner and not just someone holding your logged-in session: your password, if the account has one, and your two-factor step, if it is on. An account that only has a wallet signs with a wallet old enough to vouch for it.

You give that proof once, at the first such change. Your session is then unlocked for security changes for 30 minutes, and the settings stop asking. Signing in does not unlock anything, so a session stolen at the moment you log in is not already unlocked — except that the session which creates a new account is unlocked for an hour, so setting up a new account never asks for the password you just chose, and an account that signs in only through a social sign-in (Google, Facebook, X or Instagram, where the site has one switched on) is unlocked by signing in, because that is the only proof it has. The window does not grow when you use it; Lock now in Settings ends it early; and when anything about how the account signs in changes, the windows on your other devices close. The honest limit: someone who steals your session inside an unlocked window can make those changes until it ends — which is why it is short. The proofs that a new key really works are never skipped: turning on an authenticator still takes a code from it, making a wallet your second factor still takes that wallet's signature, and making your password the second factor takes the password itself.

“That email already belongs to another account” is a fact about somebody else, and there is no email this site could send instead — when you set an email in your settings you need to know whether it took, and a sign-up whose email was not added has to tell you. So that answer is rationed: three every 30 days, counted per account and per connection, so deleting an account and making another does not reset it. After the third, adding or changing an email (and signing up with one, from that connection) pauses until the 30 days are up, with the same answer for every address, in use or not. Someone with many connections and many invites could still ask a few times each; the ration is what keeps it from being free.

Speed limits

Nearly every action has a cap on how often it can be repeated. Making Send Calls is limited to 10 attempts every 10 minutes, comments 25, joining communities 30, and starting a community 3 per day. Logins and wallet challenges are limited per IP address. The limits sit far above what a real person does and far below what a script needs.

Uploads

The site does not trust what a file calls itself. It reads the first few bytes of every upload and checks they match a real image or video. A file named cat.png that is secretly a program fails that check.

It also strips what a phone writes inside the file. A photo or video carries where it was taken and on what device — hidden fields in a JPEG, PNG, WebP or GIF, and the tag blocks in an MP4 or WebM, which is where a phone puts the location. Every upload has those removed before it can be served, and the pixels, sound and timing are left exactly as they were: a JPEG keeps its orientation, so a phone photo is not turned on its side, and colour profiles stay. Uploaded files are served to anyone byte for byte, so a file the site cannot read well enough to clean is refused rather than published as it came.

Your wallet stays yours

This is the part people worry about most, so here it is precisely.

The site never takes custody. It never sees a private key or a recovery phrase, it cannot move your coins, and it cannot approve anything on your behalf. Linking a wallet is normally a signature, which proves ownership and nothing else. A wallet that cannot sign can be proved instead by a burn of any amount of $SEND or ETH that you send from it yourself, in your own wallet app; the site never builds or sends that transaction, it only reads it on the chain, and once linked the wallet is read-only in the same way.

There are exactly two features where the site builds a transaction, and both are swaps. The one-click swap on the home page buys $SEND or $GWC with ETH on Robinhood Chain. And every token’s on-chain detail has a 🚀 Swap in my wallet button that buys that token with ETH on Robinhood Chain or Ethereum, or with SOL on Solana — every token but a tokenized real-world asset on Robinhood Chain, as below. Either way the site builds a transaction and hands it to your wallet. You choose the amount and approve it, in your own wallet, and then your ETH or SOL really does leave. Nothing else on the site can spend anything. (A burn — to prove a wallet that cannot sign, or for a Data API key — is a transaction you make yourself, in your own wallet app; the site only reads it.)

Be precise about this

“Non-custodial” means the site cannot move your money on its own. It does not mean money never moves. A swap spends real ETH or SOL the moment you approve it in your wallet, and a swap that goes badly is still your loss.

Swap in my wallet

The button opens a small panel. You type an amount of the chain’s own coin; the server asks the exchange what that buys right now and shows you what you spend, about what you get, the least you will get, the token’s own tax and the price impact. Press Swap in my wallet and the server builds the transaction for your wallet to show, check and sign. On Robinhood Chain it goes through the same exchange router as the home-page swap; on Ethereum, through the token’s deepest ETH pool on Uniswap or SushiSwap; on Solana, through Jupiter, and your wallet signs and sends it itself. A token the site cannot route — no pool against ETH, or pools on an exchange it does not route — gets a plain “This site cannot build that swap” with the reason, not a guess.

No swap for a real-world asset on Robinhood Chain. A token that Market Data counts as a tokenized real-world asset on evidence (Section 13b) — a share, an ETF, a bond, commodity or credit fund — has no button. Where the button would be, a line says the Scanner does not offer swaps for those. The server refuses as well: asked to quote or build one, it answers that no quote or transaction is built for it, before it reads anything and whatever the amount, so the button cannot be forced. A token in Robinhood’s Stock Token registry has no button on the registry’s word, even before its contract is read on the chain. Any other token keeps the button until the chain confirms it as a real-world asset, and a token that only shares an RWA’s ticker and every token on Ethereum and Solana always keep it.

It is built to be dull on purpose. It never holds funds, never asks for a token approval, never picks the amount and never sends anything. The page checks the transaction before your wallet ever sees it: on Robinhood Chain and Ethereum, that it goes to one of the exchange routers the page knows; on Solana, that your own account pays for it and Jupiter’s program is in it. The guard against the price moving is 10%: if the price moves further than that before the swap lands, it fails instead of filling. On Robinhood Chain and Ethereum a transaction expires 20 minutes after it is built. On Solana it lasts only as long as Solana lets Jupiter’s transaction live, and after that the panel says it expired. A buy whose token keeps more than 49% as a tax is not built at all, and from 5% price impact the panel warns that your size moves the pool. The swap itself keeps nothing, not the amount and not the wallet; a signed-in member’s $SEND or $GWC buy earns the swap’s Send Power (Section 8), and the points record keeps only a keyed fingerprint of the transaction. A swap button is not a recommendation — it sits on every token but a counted real-world asset, the dangerous ones included, and the scanner’s warnings sit right beside it.

How holdings are checked

Your rewards depend on what you actually hold, so the site never asks your browser. It reads your balance straight from the blockchain on the server, where you cannot edit the answer. A check is only trusted for 26 hours. After that your bonus pauses until it is re-checked, which happens automatically whenever you open your dashboard.

That pause is deliberate. Without it, someone could get verified once, sell everything, and keep the bonus forever.

The wallet tracker

You can save wallet addresses to watch what they do. These are read from public blockchain records, they are private to your account, and they are stored scrambled. The tracker is for signed-in members: ten wallets for everyone, and more for a $GWC holder with a Diamond level (Section 9) — a hundred per level, up to a thousand.

It reads each wallet straight from the chain: every token transfer it ever sent or received, up to 20,000 of them (past that, it says the wallet has too many to read in full here), its live balances, and the receipts of its newest 5,000 transactions, which are what value its trades. The report itself is worked out in your browser. Each wallet’s history is saved, scrambled, so a later refresh reads only what is new. A first read of a busy wallet can take about an hour on the public node; it saves its place every two minutes, so a read that is cut off picks up where it stopped, and your bell rings when one you left finishes.

Three honest limits. A balance or a price it cannot read makes the total say partial, never a silent $0. A token whose trades could not all be valued says its PNL is partial, or unknown and left out of the totals. And plain ETH sent between wallets leaves no record the chain can search, so only the live ETH balance is shown.

Send Power goes down too

Everything above describes how the number grows. It also shrinks. Send Power is a balance, not a trophy — it drains a little every day you do not show up, and levels can go down as well as up.

This is the part most people will find surprising, so here is the reasoning. A score that only ever climbs stops describing anything after a year. Someone who played hard for a month and left outranks someone playing every day now, permanently, and no amount of present effort can catch a large enough head start. A leaderboard like that is a history book. Making the number decay is what keeps it a description of who is actually here.

What is taken, and when

Two days off cost nothing. After that, each day without a check-in takes 0.4% of the balance, and the rate climbs by 0.2 percentage points for every further consecutive day away. Two other things add to the same daily rate: being in read-only mode adds 1 point, and every Send Call of yours currently below its entry price adds 0.3 points, up to 1.5. Whatever the total, one day can never take more than 4%.

What stops it

The daily check-in, and nothing else. Holding coins does not stop it, and neither does having a high level. Anyone can check in, including a restricted account — read-only pauses what you can make, not your ability to defend what you already earned.

It stops two of the three components. A check-in within the 24 hours before the sweep sets the absence streak to zero and skips the underwater-call charge entirely, so on a day you show up those cost you nothing. The bad-call rate only ever applies on a day you were already away: a call that went down is a market outcome, not misconduct, and billing someone daily for one while they are actively taking part would be a far harsher rule than the one intended.

The read-only charge is the exception. It is the one component a check-in does not cancel — while restricted you pay its flat one point a day regardless. A penalty that stops costing anything the moment you tap a button is not a penalty. But it is all you pay if you keep showing up: a steady 1% a day, rather than that plus an absence rate climbing toward the ceiling.

One consequence worth stating: since a Send Call can never be closed or deleted, a call that is still below its entry price a year later still counts as underwater. Old bad calls keep making absence more expensive, permanently. They cost nothing on any day you check in.

The floors, so it cannot ruin anyone

A balance at or below 5,000 is never touched at all, and the drain can never take you through that floor or below zero. If a day's drain works out to less than one whole point it takes nothing rather than rounding up. Someone who stops playing does not have their account emptied; they settle toward the floor and stay there.

Decay only ever moves the points balance. It does not touch your Holder Boost, your Diamond level, your OG tier, your badges, your community memberships, or any call's recorded history. What you did stays on the record; what you are currently worth on the board is what moves.

Nothing happens silently

Every drain writes a negative line to your points history tagged decay, and sends you a 📉 notification saying it happened. A drop is always explained and always auditable afterwards — and the line is now visible: the Loot Log used to filter its own ledger to positive rows only, so the one line that subtracts was written and then hidden. Both halves are shown.

Both halves of the ledger are now on the dashboard. A 💀 Rekt section sits under the Quest Board and states, for each way to lose, the rule and this account’s standing against it: the decay forecast, the strike ladder with the rung you are on, what a rugged or underwater call costs, what is revoked rather than drained (a sell resets the Diamond streak, ends the OG badge for good and revokes a community’s 10×), the participation check, and the beta reset with its countdown. A 🗃️ Your record list beneath it prints everything the site tracks about the account, including the parts that count against it. Neither is behind a disclosure control: a consequence you can be surprised by has to be visible without a click.

Holder Boost — owning the coins

The more of the coin supply you hold, the bigger your bonus. Every weighted 1% of supply adds 10. $GWC counts three times as much as $SEND for the same percentage.

A bag only counts at $100. The floor exists because every reward on this page is time-based, and the balance behind it used to be checked only for “more than nothing”.   • It is measured in dollars, not tokens, so it survives a coin's own price moving.
  • Each coin is judged separately. $100 of $SEND and $100 of $GWC are two different bags clearing two different floors, and they carry two different streaks.
  • Only a sale resets a streak. The paused days never count.

Send Calls

A Send Call is a public prediction. You pick a coin, and a permanent scorecard appears on your wall tracking how it does from that moment on. It is a way of putting your reputation where your mouth is.

A call can never be edited or deleted. That is the whole point. A prediction you can delete after it goes wrong is worthless. Its best-ever result is kept forever, even if the coin later dies.

How results are counted

Results are counted in Xs. Doubling in price is 1X. Going up 340% is 3.4X. Every whole X a call crosses pays a rung, and the rungs climb in a straight line: 170 + 17 × (X − 1). So 1X pays 170, 2X pays 187, 10X pays 323, and the ladder stops at 50X, which pays 1,003. A rung is paid on a price the site can trust, not on the last trade: the lower of the live price and the median of the past hour’s readings (at least six of them; until a coin has that many, nothing is credited), and it has to hold across two consecutive readings, so a single spike cannot pay the whole ladder up to it. While the live price runs more than three times the median, nothing is credited at all and the gap is not back-paid. The pool also has to be deep enough to have paid the X for real: each rung needs $500 of pooled liquidity behind it, so a $5,000 pool can pay at most 10X however high the price goes. The scorecard’s peak and grade still record the live price; only the points wait.

That is deliberately modest, and it used to be otherwise. An earlier version paid the X times the base, so 50X paid 8,500 and the whole ladder was worth more than twenty patient calls put together. Rewarding the size of a single lucky number is not the game this is trying to be, so the ladder was flattened into a straight line. All fifty rungs together now come to 29,325 — about an eighth of what the ladder is even allowed to spend.

Holding is the main event

The ladder is the smaller prize. While a call stays in profit it keeps earning a diamond-hands hold bonus that grows faster than the Xs alone — higher and longer both count, and they compound, up to two limits: height above 50X adds nothing more, and at most half an hour of holding is credited per check (a longer gap between price readings is not paid retroactively, because nobody watched the price during it). Every call has one lifetime budget (Section 8), and it is carved on purpose: the opening award may take at most a tenth of it, the ladder at most three tenths, and at least six tenths are reserved for holding in profit.

It also pays more when you are not alone. When the people who tapped Send It on your call are themselves in profit from their own entry, your hold bonus is larger: one tenth extra per profitable Sender, up to three times. A Sender only counts toward that crew with at least $20 of the coin bought from the pool and still held in a linked wallet, read on-chain when they tap — a tap with nothing behind it is not a crew member. Senders' positions (not the caller's) are read again on-chain whenever a call's Senders list is opened, at most once every five minutes and only for the thirty most recent Senders, so a Sender who sells out drops out of the crew once that re-read catches it, not the moment they sell. That multiplier is applied to the payout, after the curve — folded into the running total instead, as it once was, a three-times crew would really have been worth 5.2 times. A conviction play that carries other people with it is worth more than a lonely one, by three, not by five.

How size is counted. The opening award scales with the position behind the call: the dollar value of the coin you bought from its pool and still hold across your linked wallets, read from the chain at the moment you call. Every $100 of that is 1× the base of 120, the floor is 1× (a call with nothing behind it still opens on the base of 120), and it stops at 100× for $10,000 or more. Tokens sent to you, or bought and then sold back to the pool, do not count, and a position is never valued above the liquidity of the pool it sits in. If the chain cannot be read, the call still goes through at 1×. Send It scales the same way on its base of 30, and pays nothing on a tap with no position.

One honest caveat about the name. “Holding” here means the price holds. The bonus is worked out from the call's current price against its entry price, and nothing re-reads the caller's wallet after the call is made. Someone who sells their whole position keeps earning the diamond-hands bonus for as long as the coin stays up. It is a name for the pattern the bonus rewards, not a claim about the person. The size you had when you called is checked on-chain and hard to fake; whether you are still holding is not checked at all.

  • A coin needs at least $2,000 of trading liquidity to be callable, and a call stops earning — milestones and hold bonus both — for as long as the pool is below that. Otherwise someone could create a fake coin, trade with themselves, and farm a huge score.
  • A pool has to be deep enough to have paid the X for real. Every X that is credited — to the ladder, to the caller's hold bonus, and to the Senders' hold bonus — needs $500 of pooled liquidity behind it at the time of the check, so a $5,000 pool can be credited for at most 10X however high its price prints. The peak on the scorecard is still recorded; the points are not.
  • You get 5 calls a day to start, counted over a rolling 24 hours so midnight is not a reset to abuse. A day where one of your matured calls doubled earns you an extra slot, up to 5. A day where they all flopped costs you one, down to a floor of 1.
  • Then your Diamond level doubles it, once per level. That earned number of 1 to 5 is multiplied by two to the power of your Diamond level (Section 9), so a level 10 holder is multiplied by 1,024. In practice the allowance runs from 1 a day, for a caller sitting on the floor with no Diamond level, to 5,120 a day. Holding the coins for two years without selling is what buys the right to call that often, and selling resets it to 1× the same day.
  • A call is only judged after 24 hours, so it gets a fair chance to run.
  • If a coin you called gets rugged — its live liquidity, as the price feed reports it, collapses below $100, whoever pulled it — the call is marked rugged for good and your daily allowance drops by 2, once per rugged call and never below the floor of 1.
  • Burning a full day's allowance inside one hour is treated as spam, and only counts if your allowance was 3 or more, so a one-call-a-day user is never punished for using it.
  • You cannot call your own coin. If a wallet you have signed with deployed the token or is its current owner, the call is refused. The owner is read from the contract’s own owner(); the deployer comes from a block explorer only when the site has a key for one (Section 16b), and otherwise from GoPlus Security’s record of who created the contract — never from anything you tell the site. Call something you do not control. If neither can be read (GoPlus names no creator and there is no keyed explorer, or the token has no owner()), the rule has nothing to match against and does not fire.
  • Several accounts on one connection calling the same coin is treated as a ring. Once three accounts behind the same connection have made a Send Call on the same coin inside seven days, every one of them goes read-only — the whole ring, not just whoever acted last — and each still climbs its own ladder (24 hours, then a week, then permanent). Buying out of that restriction costs double (Section 15). The connection is never stored in the clear: the site keeps only a blind index of it, the same kind of keyed fingerprint Section 5 describes for emails, and that index is erased from a call eight days after it was made. Sharing a connection is fine, and linking several wallets to one account is fine and always counts toward the same Send Power; several accounts pushing one coin from the same connection is what the check is looking for.

Other people can tap Send It on your call to join in. They earn from their own entry price, not yours, and it is capped at 30 a day.

Communities

Any coin can have a community. You paste its address, and if it has at least $500 of liquidity and you hold at least $100 of it, priced live, in a wallet you have linked, a page is created, automatically decorated with the coin's logo and links.

A community stays in starting-up mode until 10 verified holders have joined. Only then does it go live.

The account that started a community is paid a one-time founder award of 15,000 base Send Power when it goes live — once per account, however many communities it founds. It is a capped action like the rest of the table, so it comes out of the same shared daily budget; if that budget is already spent on the day the community goes live, the award is recorded as zero and is not paid later.

Proving you belong

To count as verified you must actually hold that community's own coin, checked on the blockchain from a wallet you signed with. This is re-checked in the background. Sell your coins and you lose your verified status and the 10× with it.

Two rules stop one person pretending to be a crowd. No more than 2 verified joins may come from the same network address, and joins from the founder's own network do not count toward their own community going live.

There is one exception to holding: the wallet that created the coin can start its community without holding any, because the chain already proves who they are.

A community also shows what share of the coin’s supply its members hold, as one figure for the whole room: the balances of members’ linked read-only wallets, summed from the chain’s own transfer ledger (or, for a token whose ledger cannot be trusted, read wallet by wallet within a fixed bound), divided by the total supply and rounded to two significant figures. It appears only once three or more members have linked a wallet, because with fewer it would be one person’s balance with extra steps. Nothing per member is ever shown; the figure says when it was last refreshed and, when it came from the ledger, the block it was read at.

Community XP and conviction

Communities level up from what their members do inside them. Members separately earn conviction, which is personal loyalty to that one community.

Conviction is earned only by doing things — there is no accrual for time. Staying a member for a year without posting leaves your conviction exactly where it was on the day you joined. It is a measure of participation wearing the word loyalty, and it is worth being clear about which of the two it actually is.

The public wall reaches the Send Wall

A community nobody can see is a community that never fills, so every public community post also appears on the main Send Wall, carrying its community's name, symbol and picture and linking back to it. Someone scrolling the front page can see a community is alive, tap through, and join on the spot if they hold the coin. The sandbox posts there too, badged as the sandbox.

The holders-only wall is the exact opposite and stays that way: those posts are filtered out in the database query itself, not hidden in the page, so they are never in the result to begin with.

Posting inside a community draws on the same allowance as posting on the wall — twelve posts in ten minutes, wherever you write them — so a community is not a second lane to the front page.

The site also writes two posts of its own per community, in the founder's name and earning nothing: one when it is started, saying how many holders it still needs, and one when it goes live. Each can only ever be written once. Because the one-time first-post bonus counts only posts you wrote to the wall yourself, the site posting in your name does not consume it.

Deciding things together

A live community can put a question to its holders, and only verified holders may open a proposal or vote. It runs in two rounds so a handful of people cannot rush something through: round one lasts three days and needs a simple majority to promote it; round two lasts two days and needs a three-quarters majority to pass. Quorum is 20% of qualified members in round one (at least 5, at most 50) and 30% in round two (at least 6, at most 60). One open proposal per person, five per community. Voting earns community XP like any other participation.

Two walls

Every live token community has a public wall that anyone can read, and a holders-only wall that only its verified holders can see. Anyone may read the public one; posting to either, and reading the private one, needs a verified holding — at least $100 of the token, read from a linked wallet on-chain and re-checked continuously, so selling it closes the private wall again. The open sandbox has no token to hold, so it has no private wall at all.

The private wall is enforced by the server on every route that can return a post — the feed itself is refused to anyone without a verified place, and a single post, its comments, its reactions and its votes all answer “not found” rather than “not allowed”, because “not allowed” would confirm the post is there. Nothing written on it appears on the Send Wall, on a profile, or in the public data feed; the only private posts the data key returns are the key holder’s own.

Holder snapshots

Any verified holder of a live community can take a holder snapshot: the full list of the coin’s holders, frozen at that moment, up to 30,000 wallets. The burn and zero addresses are not holders.

Where the list comes from depends on whether the site has a key for a block explorer (Section 16b). Without one, the snapshot is the chain’s own holder ledger — every transfer the coin has ever made, folded into balances — brought up to date and frozen at the block it is complete to. There is no second count to check that against, so the snapshot says what could be checked instead: a ledger the chain would not bring up to date is stored as partial, with the block it is as of; a coin that changes balances without transfer records cannot be rebuilt from them, and is partial too; a coin with more transfers than the server can rebuild from gives no list at all. With a keyed explorer, the snapshot pages through the explorer’s list instead and checks the walk against the explorer’s own holder count; one cut short or short of that count is partial. Either way a partial snapshot carries its reason and is never presented as complete. The sandbox has no coin, so it has no snapshots.

Send Squads

A community is a fan group anyone can read. A Send Squad is the opposite: a private group with a door on it, for people who want a room of their own.

What a squad is

Whoever starts a squad names it, writes a short bio, gives it a picture and a banner, and sets one gate, once. The gate is decided at creation and can never be changed afterwards — there is simply no way to edit it, so a squad cannot promise one bar and later move it. The person who started it owns it and cannot leave it; deleting their account closes the squad with its posts and calls. One person may start three squads a day and run ten in all.

The site runs one squad of its own, the $GWC 2% Squad: it takes two percent of the entire $GWC supply, summed across your linked wallets, to get in. It is owned by the site’s own account and verified like any other.

The gate

There are three kinds. Open — anyone with an account may join, and no balance is read. A number of tokens — hold at least that many. A share of the supply — hold at least that percentage of the token’s total supply, anywhere from 0.01% to all of it; because it is measured against the live supply, the number of tokens it takes moves with the supply. Whoever sets a gate has to pass it themselves, read from the chain before the squad is made.

Membership is read from the blockchain: the balances of the wallets you have linked, read-only, are summed and compared with the gate when you join. It is then re-checked on a schedule — every ten minutes the site re-reads a batch of the members it has checked least recently. Fall below the gate and your place is paused: the wall and the calls close, and you can join again the moment your wallets clear it. This is worth saying plainly: a schedule is not a live feed, so a member who sells can stay verified for ten minutes or more, until the check reaches them. A read the chain cannot complete decides nothing either way. A squad holds at most 500 verified members.

The private wall

Squad posts exist only for verified members. The feed is refused to everyone else, and a single post, its comments, reactions and votes answer “not found” rather than “not allowed”, for the same reason the holders-only wall does (Section 11). Nothing written there appears on the Send Wall, on anyone’s profile, or in the public data feed. Posting inside a squad draws on the same allowance as posting anywhere — twelve posts in ten minutes — and pays you the ordinary points for a post. It pays the squad nothing: a squad’s score comes from calls and conviction alone.

Calls that score for the squad

A member can make a Send Call to the squad instead of to the public wall. The call and its scorecard are private to the squad, it takes a verified place to make one, and every rule from Section 10 still applies — the daily allowance, the $2,000 of liquidity, the deployer rule, the ring and spam checks. One call per coin per squad: a public call on the same coin is a separate call, and both may stand.

The person earns nothing from a squad call

Every point a squad call ever earns is written to the squad: the opening award, every X it climbs, the hold bonus while it stays in profit, and every Send It on it. The caller’s own Send Power does not move by a single point, and neither does a Sender’s. The squad is credited flat — no Holder Boost, no OG, no community multiplier — and never for the same thing twice.

The numbers are the public ones, paid to a different ledger. The opening award is 120 times the size of the caller’s verified position (each $100 is 1×, the floor is 1×, the cap is 100×). Each whole X pays a rung of 170 + 17 × (X − 1), up to 50X, on a price the site can trust: the lower of the live price and the past hour’s median, sustained across two readings, with $500 of pooled liquidity behind every X. Holding in profit pays the same diamond-hands curve. A Send It on a squad call pays 30 times the Sender’s size, only on a real position, and the Sender’s hold bonus follows it to the squad. One lifetime budget per call, carved the same way.

All of it is behind a sign-in: signed out, every squad request is refused and the pages show a locked panel. A squad is a club, not an endorsement of the coin that gates it, and never a reason to buy one.

Rocket Run

A free game, once per day. A rocket climbs and a number rises with it. You tap to cash out whenever you like. At some hidden moment the rocket explodes, and if you have not cashed out by then you get nothing.

Nothing is at stake. There is nothing to buy, no way to pay to play again, and no way to lose anything you had. The only thing you can win is a temporary points bonus.

multiplier after s seconds = e ^ (0.06 × s) 10 seconds → 1.8× 30 seconds → 6× 60 seconds → 36× boost earned = 1 + (cash-out − 1) / 4 capped at 5× cash out at 2× → 1.25× boost cash out at 9× → 3× boost cash out at 17× → 5× boost (the cap)
The climb starts slow and accelerates. The reward for waiting is deliberately gentle, so waiting longer is mostly extra risk for very little extra bonus.

Why you cannot cheat, and neither can the site

This is the part worth understanding. When you launch, the server rolls the explosion point immediately and writes it down before you see anything. It is never sent to your browser until the round is over. Your browser is only drawing an animation; it genuinely does not know the answer.

Because the number was fixed at launch and stored, the site cannot move it either when it sees you about to win. And because the multiplier is calculated from the server's own clock, changing your computer's clock does nothing.

The honest limits of that

This makes cheating impossible for players and stops the outcome being changed mid-round. It is not the same as being mathematically provable to an outsider: you are trusting that the server rolled fairly. Since nothing is staked, there is nothing to win by cheating — but it is worth stating plainly rather than calling it provably fair.

Other rules: the explosion point is capped at 50×, a round left abandoned expires after 5 minutes and counts as a loss, and the day rolls over at midnight UTC.

The flight also ends by itself. The page keeps a line open to the server while the rocket climbs, and the server answers it the moment the flight reaches its explosion point, settling the round as a bust, so nobody plays on past it. Your day’s flight is spent the moment you launch: reloading mid-flight picks the same round back up, and never rolls a new one.

Guests, and today’s Rocket Riders board

You do not need an account to fly. Anyone signed out gets one free flight a day in their browser, once they have answered the 18+ question. It is the same flight — the explosion point rolled and kept on the server — but it pays nothing: no Send Power and no boost. What it can do is put a name on today’s Rocket Riders board.

The board is opt-in for everybody. A guest types a name; a member taps to put their @name up; nobody is listed just for flying. It shows the top 20 of the UTC day, best cash-out first, the earlier flight first in a tie, with guests in plain grey marked “guest”. A guest’s name follows the username rules and cannot be a member’s name or the site’s, lookalikes included, and a moderator can take a name down for the day. Clearing cookies is not another roll: one internet address gets at most five guest flights a day. A guest who chooses to join or sign in from the prompt, and does so within 30 minutes in the same browser, keeps the flight under their @name. Unclaimed guest flights are deleted after 30 days.

The risk scanner

New coins appear constantly, and a lot of them are traps. The Scanner page reads a coin’s public record and puts a plain-English warning label on the dangerous ones. It has two tabs.

Scan an address is open to everyone. Paste any token or pool address on Robinhood Chain, Ethereum or Solana and read the same full on-chain profile the site shows for every coin; a pool address opens the token it prices. A chain chooser sits under the box: Auto reads the chain from the address — a Solana address is Solana’s, and a 0x address is tried on Robinhood Chain, then on Ethereum — or you name one chain. A coin read on Ethereum is framed in Ethereum blue and one on Solana in Solana purple, so the chain is never a surprise. Every scan re-reads its sources and is kept for everyone — the token, the time and the count, never who scanned — so a coin someone already scanned pulls up with its latest read. On Robinhood Chain, if the chain cannot be reached, the last snapshot is shown and labelled as such; on Ethereum and Solana the page says it could not reach the source and tells you nothing it did not read. The coins other tokens are priced in — wrapped ETH and SOL, the big stablecoins, wrapped bitcoin — are refused as tokens to profile.

Robinhood Chain is read from its own node. Ethereum and Solana cannot be read that way from here — a keyless Ethereum node refuses a long walk back through its history, and Solana is a different kind of chain — so each of their readings comes from a place that can give it: pools, prices and trades from Dexscreener (up to 30 pools of the token); holders, verification, ownership, taxes and the honeypot check on Ethereum, and the mint and freeze authorities, transfer fees and LP burn on Solana, from GoPlus Security; the name, symbol, decimals and supply from that chain’s own node; and the candles and the trade tape from GeckoTerminal. The profile is built into the same shape as a Robinhood Chain one, so every card and flag reads the same way. Two checks need a chain’s whole history and stay on Robinhood Chain: the block-0 trace and the bundler read, both below. On the other chains they are marked as not read. Send Calls, the watchlist and wall pins work with Robinhood Chain coins; on the other chains the Scanner reads the profile and that is all. Every coin’s on-chain detail also carries the 🚀 Swap in my wallet button, which Section 7 describes — all but a tokenized real-world asset on Robinhood Chain.

It watches new pools land on Robinhood Chain’s Uniswap-V2-style factory — the one behind the site’s swap router — and scores each one the same way. It reads that one factory only: a launch on another venue never appears here, so a coin missing from the radar has not been judged — it has not been seen.

Any other token is scored as before until the chain confirms it as a real-world asset, and a token that only shares an RWA’s ticker and every coin on Ethereum and Solana always are.

A weakness, and how the verdict handles it now

A warning can only trip on data the site could actually read, so a coin too new to have been indexed used to score 100 out of 100 — the same as a coin that genuinely passed every check. A perfect score could mean “all clear” or “we could not see anything”, and nothing on screen told them apart.

The scanner now tracks which of six things it managed to read — indexing, liquidity, holders, concentration, verification and the early-buyer trace. If the first three are not all readable the coin is marked thin data. On Robinhood Chain, since 27 October 2026, a coin is thin data too when nobody read its contract: no reading from GoPlus Security, and nothing to say whether its source is published. A thin-data coin’s band is pulled down from “looks OK” to “caution” whatever the number says, and the “What we could not check” row names each signal that could not be read. And since 24 October 2026, on every chain, thin data is on the floor: whatever such a coin scores, a full 100 included, it never wears the 🚀 or the ✅ — it shows 🌫️ “Not enough data yet” and names what was missing. Before that date a thin-data coin that still scored 100 kept the green rocket. The number itself is still not changed, so read it beside that label: a 100 on thin data means “nothing we could read tripped”, not “all clear”.

Who bought first, and did they stay?

The deepest check on the site does something a price chart cannot: it replays the pool's own history and finds the wallets that got in before anyone could have reacted — then asks whether they are still holding, or whether they sold into the people buying now.

It looks at two windows. Block 0 is the very first block in which the pool ever paid a coin out, so its buyers got in before anyone could react. The site calls them snipers and judges what they took, still hold and sold, never who is behind a wallet. The first ten buys is the wider view, and since block 0's buyers are the earliest of them, the two windows nest.

Every early wallet is followed as a cluster rather than an address, because moving coins to a fresh wallet is the oldest way to look clean. The trace follows the wallet, everyone it sent coins to, and everyone they sent to — two hops, up to twelve destinations each, up to a hundred and fifty wallets per coin, and the thirty largest early wallets in full. Anything beyond that is counted and said out loud rather than quietly dropped.

For each cluster it reports what it took, what it still holds as a live balance read fresh from the chain, whether it kept buying afterwards, how much supply it sold that it demonstrably never bought here, and a profit-and-loss built from the actual transactions the coins moved in. Then it labels the cluster: accumulator, seller, fully out, or unknown.

Unknown is an answer, not a zero

A balance that cannot be read is stored as nothing at all, never as zero, and if any wallet in a cluster is unreadable the whole cluster reports unknown. This is the single most important rule in the whole scanner. A tool that quietly recorded an unreadable balance as “sold everything” would be inventing rugs that never happened — and it would be most likely to do it exactly when the chain was congested, which is when people are most anxious to check.

The same care governs the green verdict. A coin whose block-0 trace has not finished is not held back from the green verdict: a full 100 with nothing on the floor tripped — scored on data we could actually read, since thin data is on the floor too, on every chain, with no red power in the contract still usable (a power an owner calls stops counting once ownership is renounced to a dead address), and with the pool’s LP not left unlocked — wears the rocket and enters the Hot Feed before the trace has answered. Only a finished trace that finds the first-block buyers sold out — the −28 flag, which pulls the score below 100 — takes it away. The trace’s state is still said out loud in the panel, where “who bought first” is listed under What we could not check until it completes, and a reader who wants the finished trace before the tag can require it with the Block-0 must be clean setting. Two things do still wait for a finished, clean first block: the Best Runners record of when a coin first cleared the site’s own bar, and the scan reply the site’s optional Telegram bot posts to a group, which reads Checking block 0… until the trace is done.

That describes the bar the site sets for itself. A reader who sets filters of their own does not change the tag: it is the site’s score and nothing else. Their filters narrow the DEX List, and the Hot Feed shows only coins that both carry the tag and clear their settings, so the feed can never show something the tag would not. A score of 75 to 99 earns a second, quieter tag, Looks Good, that deliberately stops short of the word Send; below the floor neither is available at any score. The detail panel does not attribute the verdict to anyone’s settings: where there are flags to list, its heading reads Why we say, and where there are none it says plainly that no automatic red flag tripped. No setting can waive the floor: a honeypot, a coin dumping as it is read, block-0 buyers who have already sold, the risk model’s own bottom tier, thin data, a red fact that can still be used, and unlocked LP.

Two limits worth knowing. The green verdict is gated on block 0 only: the first-ten cohort is shown to you but does not change the score, so a coin with a clean first block whose next nine buyers all dumped still shows a rocket, with the contradiction visible in the panel for anyone who opens it. And the per-wallet percentages are shares of total supply while the summary and the verdict use tradeable float — two denominators on one screen, so compare like with like.

What the contract lets someone do

Every profile has a Contract card. On Robinhood Chain and Ethereum it carries GoPlus Security’s reading of the contract (GoPlus reads Robinhood Chain too); on Solana, the mint’s own authorities, read from Solana’s node. Some of what a contract allows is serious enough that a score would hide it, so it is named instead. These are the red facts: a honeypot, a balance that cannot all be sold at once, buying blocked, more tokens mintable, a blacklist, transfers that can be paused, an owner who can change anyone’s balance, a tax that can be changed or set for one wallet, trade caps that can be tightened onto sellers, a hidden owner, ownership that can be taken back, and self-destruct; on Solana, a mint or freeze authority still held. Apart from a honeypot, which GoPlus’s reading trips as the honeypot warning itself, none of them has a weight in the score. But on every chain, since 24 October 2026, a red fact that can still be used holds back both the 🚀 and the ✅: the coin reads caution, and the verdict names each fact and who read it.

A real renounce changes that. Seven of those powers are ones an owner calls: minting, the blacklist, pausing, changing balances, changing the tax, a per-wallet tax and changing the caps. Once ownership sits at a dead address — the zero address, the usual burn address that ends in dEaD, or a third burn address common on launchpads, addresses nobody holds a key to — nobody can call them, so the card still lists each one but marks it as renounced, and it no longer holds anything back. A renounce only counts when it is real: never over a proxy, whose code whoever administers it can swap, and never where GoPlus reads a way to take ownership back or a hidden owner. A honeypot, a sell or buy block, self-destruct, a hidden owner and a take-back path stay red whoever the owner is. On Robinhood Chain the owner is read from the contract’s own owner() first. A live owner is shown in full with a link to it, marked as a wallet or a contract, read from the chain.

The pool’s liquidity gets the same treatment. On Robinhood Chain the site reads on the chain how much of the pool’s LP tokens sit at dead addresses — burned, gone for good — leaving out the small permanent minimum every such pool locks away. On Ethereum it uses GoPlus’s list of the ten largest LP holders, and only when GoPlus read the same pool. If less than half of the LP is burned or in a locker, the coin carries 🔓 LP not burned or locked: whoever holds the rest can pull it. That is a caution flag, and it holds back the 🚀 and the ✅ like a red fact. It is raised only when both shares were actually read for that pool; an unread share raises nothing and passes nothing. A renounce does not touch it, because the LP is not an owner’s power. On Solana only GoPlus’s burned share for the main pool is read, with no locker share, so the 🔓 flag is never raised there: the card shows the burned share alone, and a Solana coin without the 🔓 has not passed this check.

Bundlers

On Robinhood Chain every coin’s detail also lists its bundlers: wallets that bought from the pool and moved tokens in the same block. A bundle is one of three things — a wallet that bought and, in that same block, sent tokens on to other wallets; one transaction that bought and also moved somebody’s own tokens; or a contract that split one buy across several wallets. Most buys pass through a router, and many pay a trading bot’s fee wallet on the way, so neither of those is ever counted as a bundle: a contract that carried buys for three or more different buyers is a router, and a wallet that took a side slice beside three or more different traders is a fee wallet. What each bundler still holds comes from the chain, and what it paid and got back comes from the pool’s own swaps in WETH or USDG — no historical dollar price is guessed. A history too long to read in full here says so, and a figure that could not be read shows as “—”, never 0. Bundlers are shown, not scored: they do not move the number.

Finding a coin by its name

The Scanner’s box, the menu search and the DEX List also take a name or a symbol, 2 to 40 characters. The server asks GeckoTerminal’s pool search and Dexscreener’s search, both kept to Robinhood Chain, and the site’s own records, with only the words typed, and lists up to 25 tokens whose name or symbol contains them — traded today or not — each with its market cap, 24-hour volume, liquidity and holders. When there may be more, the list says these are only the first matches. Holders come from the chain’s own ledger, a recent read, or GoPlus, which is asked about at most four tokens a search; one not read in time says so, and the page asks once more. What it cannot find is a coin with no pool anywhere: neither GeckoTerminal nor Dexscreener lists one, and the search does not ask the block explorer.

Where the data comes from

On Robinhood Chain: the chain’s own node (balances, pools, the holder ledger, the LP burn, the block-0 trace), a price service (Dexscreener), and GoPlus Security, an automated contract scanner whose readings supply the contract’s powers, whether its source is published, who created it, and the holder count until the site’s own on-chain count for that coin has been built. On Ethereum and Solana: Dexscreener, GoPlus Security, GeckoTerminal and each chain’s own node. A block explorer’s API is read only when the site has a key for one (Section 16b); without one, whatever only the explorer could give is said as not read. GoPlus’s readings are one vendor’s automated output, not an audit. The score is a set of quick heuristics on public data. It is not an audit, not a guarantee, and not advice.

Top chains

This tab ranks chains by TVL, DEX volume, liquidity, market cap or fees, highest or lowest first, over 24 hours, 7 days, 30 days or a custom run of whole UTC days. It lists every chain DefiLlama lists with a TVL or a DEX volume, and Robinhood Chain always. A chain with no figure for the ranking goes last whichever way the list runs. Where a plain reading could mislead, it says so beside the figure — “pools created, not tokens or contracts”, for one. Robinhood Chain shows no market cap at all, because it has no token of its own: gas is paid in ETH.

A chain’s market cap, checked twice

A chain’s market cap is the market cap of the chain’s own token. The figure shown is CoinGecko’s, for every listed chain, and it is checked against a second, independent source, CoinLore. A CoinLore coin counts as the same coin when, on the same read, it has the same symbol, the same name and a price within 10% of CoinGecko’s, so a different coin that shares a name is left out. Two figures within 10% of each other agree; when they do not, the page says why from the numbers — the same price but a different circulating supply counted, or how far apart the prices are. CoinGecko is also checked against itself: its figure has to match its own price times its circulating supply to within 2%, it may not be larger than its own fully diluted value, and a figure CoinGecko last updated more than six hours before the read is marked as stale.

A coin CoinLore lists under another name — it still calls BNB “Binance Coin” and Cronos “Crypto.com Chain” — counts by its symbol and price alone, on every chain, under a tighter rule: its price must be within 3% of CoinGecko’s on the same read, and it must be the only coin with that symbol priced within 10% among the coins read (CoinLore’s first 2,000 for a chain, its 100 largest on the bubble map). Two such coins and neither counts. 3% is a third of the name match’s band: on the samples the site measured on 28 and 30 October 2026, the same coin’s two prices on one read were at most 2.51% apart. Wherever the figure is shown, its chip adds “by symbol and price” and its title names CoinLore’s coin; the full check — in the chain’s view, or in the coin’s card on the bubble map — gives CoinLore’s name beside CoinGecko’s and how far apart the two prices are, so you can judge it. The daily walk that finds such a coin compares prices up to half an hour apart, so it accepts it within 10%; every 30-minute read then holds it to 3% on prices read together, and a coin that drifts further apart is left out of that read, with the reason said (and the gap, while it is within 10%). A walk of the list that was cut short adds no such coin until the walk is whole, since a second one could be further on. A different coin that only shares the ticker is still left out.

CoinLore’s own list can describe a coin oddly, and the check says what it read rather than guess. On 3 November 2026 its first 2,000 coins held no $LTC: its coin with Litecoin’s 84 million maximum supply was listed as “x”, symbol $X, at $0.000045. So the check finds no CoinLore coin for $LTC, and nothing is matched on a guess.

DefiLlama publishes market caps too, but they relay CoinGecko’s figure, so the site never counts them as a second source; one stands in only when CoinGecko cannot be read, and says so. A CoinGecko market cap of 0 means CoinGecko lists no circulating supply for that coin, so CoinGecko has no figure for it, never $0. CoinLore’s is shown instead, marked as the only source, when it has a matching coin, and the page says the figure is not available only when neither does. CoinPaprika is not used: its free plan is for personal use only. Until 28 October 2026 the page took DefiLlama’s figures, for the 120 chains with the most TVL only.

Top tokens

This tab ranks the most-traded tokens of every chain the site reads — Robinhood Chain, Ethereum, Solana, Base, BNB Chain, Arbitrum, Polygon, Avalanche and OP Mainnet — together by default, or one chain’s, picked from a drop-down that shows each chain’s logo and name. You can rank by volume, liquidity, market cap, fully diluted value or price change, over 5 minutes, an hour, 6 hours, 24 hours or a custom run of days. The list is drawn from GeckoTerminal’s most-traded pools: up to 200 on Robinhood Chain, read every ten minutes, and up to 60 on each other chain, read every hour. So “lowest” means lowest within those lists, never the whole chain’s.

Two flags keep the ranking honest. Thin is under $10,000 of liquidity. Wash is 24-hour volume over 100 times the liquidity, and wash tokens are left out of the highest-volume list by default, with the page saying how many it left out and a button to put them back. A market cap larger than the chain’s own fully diluted value is a figure counted across several chains, so it is kept out of the market-cap ranking and listed last. A pool reserve GeckoTerminal gives as 0 is read as not known, never as $0. Opening a token shows its pools, its links, a week of hourly candles, and, at the bottom, the Scanner’s own profile of it — a read that is not added to the Scanner’s public list of recent scans.

RWAs

The third tab lists Robinhood Chain’s real-world-asset tokens — tokenised shares, ETFs, bond, commodity and credit funds — and decides what counts as one on evidence, never on a name. The evidence it accepts: the address is listed as a Robinhood Chain deployment in Robinhood’s own Stock Token registry, and the token’s beacon slot, read on the chain, names Robinhood’s token registry contract. A token the registry lists counts on the registry’s word even before that slot is read, and is marked “Registry only” until the slot names the registry; a slot that names something else is flagged as a fingerprint mismatch. A token the registry no longer lists counts only when that slot names the registry and its code is the very code the registry’s own tokens run, because any contract can write that address into its slot. A few other issuers are on a list the site keeps by address — Ondo Global Markets, Anchored, ST0x and Midas — and each of their tokens is confirmed only once the chain shows its issuer’s own beacon (for Midas, a live proxy). Until then it is shown, marked as not confirmed, and left out of every total.

A name or a ticker never qualifies: a token that only shares an RWA’s ticker is a lookalike, named on the real one’s card and never listed, and its own pages say where the real one is. Fiat-backed stablecoins, and tokens that are merely paired with an RWA, are listed apart and never counted. Each RWA shows its value on chain (its supply read on the chain times its DEX price — not the company’s value), its market cap, liquidity and 24-hour volume from GeckoTerminal, its TVL in DeFi from DefiLlama, and its premium or discount: its DEX price against a reference price, from Chainlink’s feed read on the chain or else Robinhood’s own quote. The issuer’s powers are listed as well, read from the registry’s own records — minting, burning, pausing, blocking wallets, changing the multiplier, upgrading every token at once. The Scanner shows no health score for a token counted as an RWA here, and offers no swap for it (Sections 13 and 7). The site is not affiliated with Robinhood Markets, Inc., and uses none of Robinhood’s logos or artwork.

The whole market, as bubbles

Above the tabs sits a bubble map of the whole crypto market over the last 24 hours: one bubble for each of CoinGecko’s 100 largest coins by market cap, on every chain. A bigger market cap makes a bigger bubble on a compressed scale — a bubble’s area grows with the square root of its market cap — so the middle and the small coins stay big enough to read, and no bubble is ever smaller than its ticker needs: every ticker shows. A bubble held at its ticker’s size is no longer sized by its market cap, so on a narrow screen a coin with a long ticker can be drawn bigger than larger coins, and the caption counts how many are held. The three largest coins are always the three biggest bubbles, in order.

Colour is the coin’s 24-hour price change in nine steps, from up more than 10% to down more than 10%; a coin with no change given is hatched, never drawn as 0%, and a coin with no market cap is listed but not drawn. Dominance — a coin’s share of the whole market — is worked out only when CoinGecko’s coin figures and its market total were read within two hours of each other. Each market cap is checked against CoinLore by the same rules as a chain’s, the match by symbol and price included, among CoinLore’s 100 largest coins. The map can show the top 50 or the top 100, leave out the coins CoinGecko files as stablecoins, fold away, or be read as a list, which is also the keyboard’s way in. It is a picture of the last day, read every ten minutes, and not a tip about any coin on it.

How it is collected

The figures are gathered on a timetable, not when you look, so opening the page never waits on the outside services: Robinhood Chain’s pulse every minute; the chains’ figures and market caps every 30 minutes; the bubble map, Robinhood Chain’s pools and the radar’s new pairs every ten minutes; the other chains’ pools every hour; the RWAs every 30 minutes, each part only when it is due. The radar’s background read only gathers new pairs and their market figures; it judges nothing, and the checks still run when a member has the radar open. The footer of the page names every source.

Alerts

The notification says who posted and what kind of thing it was. It does not carry the post’s text. Help-desk questions and holders-only community posts are never broadcast at all.

An alert turns one person’s action into up to two hundred notification rows, so it is bounded in five places: two hundred subscribers to a wall, fifty walls to an account, three alerts from any one author per bell per hour, four fan-outs per author per hour, and three alerts on one wall from any one network. Each of those refuses out loud when it is reached rather than accepting the subscription and quietly never delivering — a switch reading ON that never rings would be a lie, and it is the specific failure this design was written to avoid. Alert notifications are also kept in their own bucket, so a busy wall can never push a level-up or a restriction notice out of your bell.

Visitors per day are counted without keeping anyone. At most 90 days are shown. Everything here is a total: no id, name or per-person row ever leaves the server.

The bot catcher

A points game invites scripts. The site watches how fast accounts create things and whether they post the same thing repeatedly. Accounts acting faster than a human is capable of are put into read-only mode.

Read-only is not a ban. You can still browse everything, check in daily, connect a wallet, buy and hold coins, and keep earning from your Holder Boost. What stops is creating things: posting (on the wall or in a community), commenting, reacting, voting, following, calling and Sending It on others’ calls, tracking wallets, uploading images or video, customising your profile or wall, pinning tokens to it, joining or starting a community, taking a holder snapshot, opening or voting on a proposal, claiming an easter egg, minting an API key, and flying Rocket Run. Connecting a wallet still works, but linking one while restricted does not pay the wallet-link points.

It does still cost points, and that is worth saying plainly. A restriction adds a flat one percentage point a day to Send Power decay (Section 8b), and it is the only part of decay that a check-in does not cancel. Keep showing up and that 1% a day is the whole of it; ignore the site while restricted and the absence rate stacks on top and climbs toward the four-percent ceiling.

The check-in is deliberately left open, and the reasoning is worth stating because the opposite choice is tempting. Gating it would make a mute compounding: the one action that defends your balance would be the one action you are barred from, so the penalty would grow every day whatever you did. That is a different and much harsher thing than pausing what you can create, and it punishes the wrong thing — a muted account that keeps turning up is behaving exactly as you would want. The flat 1% is what keeps the restriction meaningful without making it a trap.

The punishment escalates: 24 hours the first time, one week if it happens again, and permanent after that. The thresholds are set far above ordinary enthusiastic use.

One design choice you should judge for yourself

A restriction can be lifted early by buying and holding $SEND: $25 worth per 24 hours of the restriction, or a flat $1,000 to clear a permanent one — twice that if the restriction was for running a ring of accounts on one coin (Section 10). One case costs double: when three or more accounts on one connection make Send Calls on the same coin inside a week, the whole ring is restricted at once — each account on its own strike ladder — and buying out of that restriction costs $50 per 24 hours, or $2,000 for a permanent one. Every lift then starts a holding period, not just the permanent one — a timed restriction must be held out for its own length, a permanent one for 40 days. Sell during it and a timed restriction returns with its timer doubled, and a permanent one goes back to permanent. During that period an account with no linked wallet counts as having sold, so keep the wallet that holds the bag linked; a chain read that simply fails is skipped, not counted. The money goes into the coin, not to the site, and the coins stay yours. It is still a mechanic where spending money removes a penalty, and anyone reading this should weigh that for themselves rather than take our framing for it.

Strikes are also permanent. Nothing ages them out or forgives them, so three offences years apart still reach the permanent tier. There is an appeal, and it is free: the address SendRH@Atomicmail.io is carried inside every restriction object the server builds and rendered as a mailto link in the banner a restricted account sees. A person reads it. This paragraph previously said no appeal route existed, which was false in the direction that made the site look worse than it is — it told a restricted user their only way out was $1,000 while a free one sat in front of them. The buy-out is the fast, automatic route; the appeal is the one that costs nothing.

How it is built

The site is deliberately simple. There is no framework, no build step and no bundler. It is plain JavaScript that runs in the browser exactly as written, and a single Node.js program on the server.

  • One server program holds every rule: one main file, with a few modules beside it for the larger parts — Market Data, the scans of other chains, the contract facts, the swap builder and Sendy’s brain. Where a page shows a rule the server also applies, such as the risk weights or the red facts, a check or a test holds the two to the same answer. Having one source of truth means the number you see and the number you are paid cannot drift apart.
  • SQLite, built into Node itself, stores everything in a single file on disk.
  • No JavaScript is written inline in any page, which is what allows the strict security policy in section 6.
  • Only four outside code libraries are chosen directly (they bring a few dozen small packages of their own): one for checking wallet signatures, one for drawing QR codes, one HTTP client for reading the chain and the feeds, and Anthropic’s own client for Sendy, which is used only when a model key is set (Section 14b).

The advantage of this style is that anyone can read it. There is no compilation between what is written and what runs, so the code in the repository is the code in your browser. For a project asking people to trust it, that is worth more than convenience.

16b

Every request names the site

Every request the server makes to anyone else — the chain’s node, every price and data feed, the picture hosts, the sign-in providers, Sendy’s model when there is one, the Telegram bot — carries one plain identity: JustSendIt/1.0 (+https://sendrh.com), the site’s name and where to read about it (the one stock-quote read adds, inside those brackets, what it is asking for). The site never dresses itself up as a web browser. A source that will only answer a browser is not asked as one: it answers this, or its figure is shown as not read. A test fails if a browser’s identity string appears anywhere in the server’s code.

The same rule decides what the block explorer is asked. Robinhood Chain’s public explorer meets a server with a bot challenge, and the site does not try to get past one. So the explorer’s API is asked only when the operator has given the site an API key for it, and as of November 2026 the live site has none. Without it the chain answers directly wherever it can, and what only the explorer could give is said as not read:

  • whether a contract’s source is published comes only from GoPlus Security’s reading, and the contract’s own name, which only its published source gives, is not read at all; on Robinhood Chain a coin whose contract nobody read is thin data (Section 13);
  • a coin’s deployer comes from GoPlus’s record of who created it (Section 10);
  • a community’s holder snapshot freezes the chain’s own holder ledger (Section 11).

Links you press to open a coin or a wallet on the explorer still go there. That is your own browser visiting it, not the site asking.

Honest limits

A white paper that only lists strengths is an advert. These are the real limits.

  • A risk score only counts what could be read. Section 13 describes how that is handled: a coin whose liquidity, holders or index entry could not be read — or, on Robinhood Chain, whose contract nobody read — is thin data, held back from the 🚀 and the ✅ on every chain and shown as 🌫️ “Not enough data yet” with what was missing named. But the number itself is unchanged, and even a coin that clears every check is only a coin that cleared the checks the site can make. It is the most important caveat on the page.
  • Only a counted real-world asset goes without a score. A tokenized share or fund loses its score only once Market Data counts it on evidence (Sections 13 and 13b): a token in Robinhood’s Stock Token registry on the registry’s word, any other only once the chain confirms it. Until then that token keeps its score, which does not count its issuer’s powers; the page says so beside it.
  • The site depends on outside services. Prices and pools come from Dexscreener and GeckoTerminal; contract details from the chain itself and an automated contract scanner (GoPlus); Ethereum and Solana scans from those chains’ own nodes as well; the swap button’s Solana swaps from Jupiter; Market Data’s figures from DefiLlama, L2BEAT, growthepie, CoinGecko, CoinLore, Robinhood’s Stock Token list and Chainlink’s feeds. A block explorer is asked only with a key, so without one some things stay unknown and say so (Section 16b). On Robinhood Chain holder counts are read from the chain’s own Transfer events, folded into balances and stamped with the block they were read at, with the scanner, and a keyed explorer where there is one, as fallbacks; on Ethereum and Solana they are GoPlus Security’s — and every count says which source it came from. If those are wrong or offline, the site shows less, and it says so rather than inventing numbers.
  • Two sources agreeing is a check, not proof. Market Data’s figures can be late, wrong or missing. Each one says where it came from and when, and a missing one is shown as missing, but nobody here audits the sources. A coin CoinLore lists under another name is matched by its symbol and price alone, so a different coin at almost the same price could pass; the check names both coins, so you can tell.
  • Holder checks are not live. Holder boosts, community places and squad places are re-read on a schedule (every 5 to 10 minutes), so someone who sells keeps them until the next sweep reaches them — minutes, sometimes longer. The posting pass behind the menu bar’s verified count is not re-read at all (Section 14c).
  • Sendy can be wrong. He answers from the site’s own text and public data, he is not retrained, and a program that matches questions to passages will sometimes match the wrong one. Check the page he points to.
  • The numbers in the menu bar are totals, with gaps. Views have been counted only since 13 October 2026, the live count starts again from zero whenever the server restarts, and the verified chart is not a history of past counts (Section 14c).
  • Encryption protects a stolen database, not a stolen server. If an attacker took the key as well as the data, the scrambling would not save you.
  • Sign in with Send rests on this site. It is one server, not a network: if the site is down, nobody can sign in to an app with it, and an app has to trust this site’s word about who you are. An app that was handed your details can keep what it was handed; Revoke stops it getting more.
  • Rocket Run is fair but not provably fair. You are trusting the server's roll. Nothing is staked, so nothing is lost either way.
  • Points are not money. They cannot be cashed out, sold or transferred, and they never will be.
  • Nothing here is advice. The site shows what the blockchain says. What you do about it is yours to decide, and most new coins go to zero.
The point of all this

Crypto is full of sites that promise safety and deliver marketing. This one tries the opposite: state the rules exactly, show the real numbers, admit what it cannot see, and never tell you what to buy. Whether that is enough is for you to judge — which is the whole idea.
